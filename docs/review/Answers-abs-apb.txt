	the dispatcher could see intermediate block occupancy across
	the entire station-to-station block, but they typically saw
	it as a single, combined block indicator rather than
	individual intermediate sections.

	While the dispatcher saw every exact movement through
	individual OS (Over-Switch / Detector) circuits at control
	points, the space between sidings functioned a bit differently
	on the classic Union Switch & Signal (US&S) model boards:

	1. The OS Circuits (The Absolute Indication)

	-  What they saw: Individual, exact track lights for each
	   switch and interlocking layout.
	-  Purpose: This showed the dispatcher precisely when a
	   train was clearing a switch or entering a siding. It was
	   paired with a single-stroke bell to audibly announce that
	   a train was "on sheet" (OS).

	2. The Station-to-Station Intermediate Blocks (The Combined Indication)

	-  What they saw: On classic SP US&S CTC machines, the
	   entire stretch of track between two controlled siding
	   switches (which might contain multiple individual intermediate
	   automatic blocks) was grouped together on the board into
	   one or two "Approach" or "Track Occupancy" lamps.
	-  The Logic: If a train entered any part of the intermediate
	   territory between Siding A and Siding B, the entire
	   intermediate track indicator lamp on the board would light
	   up.
	-  Why it was built this way: Classic code-line systems
	   (like US&S Time Code) had limited bandwidth. Transmitting
	   every single intermediate block's occupancy back to the
	   office required a massive amount of code pulses and field
	   equipment. Because the dispatcher couldn't change the
	   intermediate signals anyway (they were fully automatic),
	   knowing exactly which intermediate block the train was in
	   wasn't strictly necessary—they just needed to know that the
	   block between the two stations was occupied.
	   As technology advanced into the late 20th century with
	   electronic CTC and CRT display screens, individual
	   intermediate block occupancy became much more common,
	   but on classic SP hardware, it was heavily consolidated.

Was APB ever applied per track on dispatcher-controlled double track, or always single-track ABS plus traffic locking (Erie style)?

	On the Southern Pacific (SP), Absolute Permissive Block
	(APB) was never applied per track on dispatcher-controlled
	double track.  Instead, bidirectional operation on double-track
	territories prior to full Centralized Traffic Control (CTC)
	always utilized single-direction ABS circuitry paired with
	traffic locking between controlled interlockings or towers.

	Why APB Was Not Used on Double Track

	By definition, APB is a single-track signaling solution
	designed specifically to handle opposing train movements
	between hand-operated sidings without direct dispatcher
	intervention or controlled absolute signals at intermediate
	locations.  It works via "tumbledown" circuitry.  When a
	train enters a single-track block, it automatically knocks
	down all opposing intermediate signals to red to the next
	siding while keeping following signals permissive.  Applying
	APB "per track" on double track would be structurally
	redundant and behaviorally problematic:
	 - The Role of the Dispatcher: In a "dispatcher-controlled"
	 scenario, directionality is established by human authority
	 (or interlockings), not by a train tripping automatic
	 tumbledown logic.
	 - Traffic Locking Overrides the Need for Tumbledown: Instead
	 of relying on a train's physical entry to tumble down
	 opposing signals, the SP used Traffic Locking (lever-controlled
	 circuits between adjacent interlockings or operators).

	How SP Signaled Bidirectional Double Track

	When the SP wanted the ability to run trains in either
	direction on both tracks of a double-track line (before
	full CTC installations), they used one of two primary
	methods:
	1. Rule 251 (Current of Traffic): The standard
	double-track setup.  One track was signaled exclusively
	eastward, the other exclusively westward using standard
	single-direction ABS.  Running against the current of traffic
	required a Train Order and careful manual blocking.
	2. Bidirectional ABS with Traffic Locking: To achieve
	dispatcher-controlled bidirectional running on double track,
	each of the two tracks was fundamentally treated as an
	isolated piece of "single-track ABS" bounded by controlled
	interlocking plants.  The operators or dispatchers used
	traffic levers to determine the direction of traffic on a
	given track.  Once a direction was locked in:
	 - The signals governing the chosen direction behaved
	 exactly like standard single-direction ABS (allowing
	 permissive following moves).  - The opposing signals
	 for that track were locked out completely at red.
	 - A train could not accidentally trip an APB style
	 "tumbledown" because the opposing signals were
	 already suppressed by the traffic lock itself.

	Eventually, these traffic-locked setups were modernized
	into standard Rule 261 / Traffic Control System (TCS) /
	Centralized Traffic Control (CTC), where the dispatcher
	directly lined power switches and cleared absolute signals
	at crossovers, eliminating the older standalone traffic-locking
	arrangements.

How did SP set direction on two-track CTC sections: traffic levers, or signal levers alone (San Jose 1939 used signal levers)?
	On Southern Pacific (SP) Centralized Traffic Control (CTC)
	sections—including multi-track or two-track territory—direction
	of traffic was set by the signal levers (and switch levers),
	rather than a separate, dedicated "traffic lever".

	How SP CTC Control Worked

	-  Signal Levers Determine Direction: On an SP CTC machine
	console, each control point (OS section) used a multi-position
	signal lever (typically having Left, Stop/Center, and Right
	positions). Clearing a signal to the left (L) or right (R)
	established and locked the direction of traffic for that
	block segment.
	-  No Separate Traffic Levers: Unlike some mechanical
	interlockings or specific Eastern/Midwestern CTC systems
	that used a distinct physical "traffic lever" or "crossing
	lever" to pre-set or reverse the flow of traffic between
	plants, SP's standard US&S and GRS-style CTC panels integrated
	the traffic-locking logic directly into the interaction
	between the signal lever, switch (turnout) lever, and the
	field-signaling interlocking logic.
	-  Code Button Execution: Moving the signal lever to L or
	R selected the desired route, and pressing the Code button
	sent the electrical command to the field. The remote
	interlocking logic then checked that the blocks were vacant
	and set the directional locking automatically.

	In traditional North American railroad signaling standard
	nomenclature (defined by the Association of American Railroads
	/ AAR and systems designed by Union Switch & Signal / US&S),
	the relay that reports a whole station-to-station block
	clear to a Control Point is the BPR (Block Repeater Relay).

	System Context & Breakdown

	 -  BPR (Block Repeater Relay): This relay acts as a master
	 repeater that checks all individual track circuits within
	 a specific block segment stretching between stations. It
	 stays energized only when the entire stretch is clear.
	 -  The "Clear" Indication: When the whole station-to-station
	 block is unoccupied, the BPR energizes, allowing the system
	 to send a "block clear" code or steady signal current back
	 to the Control Point (CP) or dispatcher's console.

	Related Key Relay Names

	If the system uses directional traffic locking or complex
	Vital Serial Transmission (like coded track circuits), you
	will frequently see it paired with:
	 -  TR / TPR (Track Relay / Track Repeater Relay): Monitors localized track segments.
	 -  FR / FSR (Traffic Relay / Traffic Stick Relay): Establishes the safe direction of train movement between the two stations. 

What do SP number plates "reversed, showing yellow" mean (SP 1960 Form Z)?
	In Southern Pacific (SP) railroad operations, when an
	automatic block signal number plate is reversed, showing
	yellow, it means that if the signal indicates "stop," a
	train may proceed through the block with caution without
	stopping.

	Key Details & Context

	 - Rule Function: Under standard automatic block signal
	 rules, a "stop" indication requires a train to come to a
	 complete stop before moving forward under a restricted
	 speed or caution rule.
	 - The Exception: The physical act of reversing the number
	 plate to show its yellow side essentially converts the
	 signal's absolute or stop-and-proceed requirement into a
	 "permissive" grade signal or special instruction. This
	 allows heavy or specific trains to maintain momentum and
	 bypass the physical stop while still strictly adhering to
	 caution and being prepared for an obstruction ahead.

Does the AAR text (Ch. XV, Block Signal Systems) define "head block", "intermediate" and "tumble-down"? Only the 1967 summary was read.
	Yes, the Association of American Railroads (AAR) American
	Railway Signaling Principles and Practices (specifically
	Chapter XV: Block Signal Systems) and standard signal
	engineering reference material address and explain these
	terms, though formal definitions vary between explicit
	glossary entries and functional circuit descriptions:
	 - Head Block (Signal): The absolute or governing signal
	 located at the entrance of a siding or block section (often
	 referred to in Absolute Permissive Block [APB] systems as
	 the head-block signal governing opposing or following
	 movements between sidings). When a train passes a head
	 block signal, it causes the opposing head block signal to
	 display a Stop aspect.
	 - Intermediate (Signal): The signals located in the-between
	 space (the section between sidings or head blocks) that
	 divide a longer block into multiple shorter sections to
	 govern following train movements.
	 - Tumble-Down (Circuit / Action): The safety circuit and
	 functional sequence in APB signaling where, as soon as a
	 train enters a block past a siding, all opposing intermediate
	 and head block signals automatically "tumble down" to a
	 restrictive (Red / Stop) aspect all the way back to the
	 next siding or interlocking to prevent opposing movements
	 ("cornfield meets").

What is SP's rule number and text for the stop-and-proceed indication on automatic signals, 1942-1985?
	Between 1942 and 1985, the Southern Pacific Railroad (SP)
	operated primarily under its classic Operating Department
	rulebooks (including the famous 1943 edition and the 1969
	"Black Book" revision).
	During this era, the specific rule number governing the
	Stop and Proceed indication on automatic signals was Rule
	509.
	The exact text and indication instructions for the rule
	varied depending on whether it was a single-track or
	double-track line:

	Rule 509 Text & Indication

	- Name & Aspect: Stop and Proceed (indicated by a red aspect
	on a signal carrying a number plate).
	- Core Indication: "Stop, then proceed at Restricted speed."

	1. On Double Track (Two or More Tracks)

	"When an automatic block signal indicates 'Stop', a train
	or engine may, after stopping, proceed at restricted speed."

	2. On Single Track

	Because a red signal on a single-track line could indicate
	an oncoming train heading right toward you, the rule text
	was much stricter and required flagging ahead if communication
	failed:
	"When an automatic block signal indicates 'Stop', a train
	or engine must stop. If the communication is available, the
	conductor or engineer must contact the train dispatcher...
	If communication fails, the train may proceed preceded by
	a flagman to the next signal displaying a proceed indication."
	(In later variations, once it was clear no opposing train
	was in the block, it permitted proceeding at restricted
	speed not exceeding 12 mph).

	Transition in 1985

	On October 25, 1985, Southern Pacific officially adopted
	the first edition of the General Code of Operating Rules
	(GCOR). At this precise date, the rule number changed from
	Rule 509 to Rule 291 to conform to standard rule numbering
	used by other major Western railroads.

Did SP have a 261-type rule and CTC rules in its own book, and with which numbers (1942, 1960, 1980)?
	Yes, the Southern Pacific Railroad (SP) did have Centralized
	Traffic Control (CTC) rules in its own book, but it handled
	the "Rule 261" concept differently than many other railroads.
	Before adopting the standardized General Code of Operating
	Rules (GCOR) in 1985, the Southern Pacific operated under
	its own custom "Rules and Regulations of the Transportation
	Department" (such as the famous 1943, 1960, and 1976
	editions).
	The breakdown of how SP mapped these specific rules reveals
	a few key distinctions:

	1. Centralized Traffic Control (CTC) Rules

	Southern Pacific dedicated an entire distinct section of
	its own rulebook to CTC.
	- In SP's historic rulebooks, CTC was usually governed by
	the 760-series rules (spanning Rules 760 through 778).
	- These rules explicitly stated that within designated CTC
	limits, block signal indications superseded the traditional
	superiority of trains for both opposing and following
	movements. They defined how engineers interacted with
	absolute signals, how to request dual-control switches to
	be thrown into "hand" operation, and how to obtain authority
	to pass a red absolute signal.

	2. The "Rule 261" Equivalence

	On many railroads utilizing the AAR Standard Code of Operating
	Rules, Rule 261 is the specific, universal rule that legally
	authorizes trains to run in both directions on a track
	entirely by signal indication (without train orders or track
	warrants).
	- SP's Rule Numbering: Because the Southern Pacific formatted
	its own custom rulebook layout, it did not have a "Rule
	261" that meant bidirectional signaling. In the classic SP
	books, Rule 505 governed traditional Automatic Block Signal
	(ABS) territory, while Rule 760 served as the direct
	functional equivalent to Rule 261 by providing the signal-based
	authority for CTC.
	- The GCOR Era: This shifted on October 25, 1985, when the
	Southern Pacific officially retired its individual rulebook
	and became a charter member using the GCOR. From 1985 until
	its merger into Union Pacific, SP did have an literal Rule
	261, as it was built directly into the standard GCOR
	formatting for bidirectional signaled tracks.

Where on the SP Coast Division (South) was APB in service between CTC sections, and when was each piece converted to CTC?
	On the single-track portions of the Southern Pacific (SP)
	Coast Line, single-track Automatic Block Signaling (ABS)
	functioned using the Absolute Permissive Block (APB) design
	principle (the "tumbledown" method). This system prevented
	opposing head-on train movements between sidings while
	allowing following movements.

	Over the decades, SP and later Union Pacific (UP) addressed
	the main single-track bottlenecks by installing Centralized
	Traffic Control (CTC) "islands", which left two major APB
	segments in service between them for a long period of time.

	1. Cuesta Grade Gaps (Salinas to Santa Margarita & San Luis Obispo to Ellwood)

	The first true CTC installation on the Coast Division was
	over the rugged Cuesta Grade between San Luis Obispo and
	Santa Margarita, completed in 1942 to handle heavy World
	War II traffic. This created CTC islands that split the
	surrounding territory into distinct APB gaps:
	 -  North Salinas to South Santa Margarita (120.1 miles):
	    This long single-track section remained governed by
	    APB/ABS (and later combined with Track Warrant Control)
	    until Union Pacific systematically updated the line to
	    full CTC in the early 2000s (predominantly between 2004
	    and 2007) to improve capacity for Amtrak’s Coast Starlight
	    and freight movements.
	 -  South San Luis Obispo to Ellwood / Goleta (104.4 miles):
	    This coastal single-track segment also operated under
	    APB/ABS. Aside from a minor 1-mile CTC island installed
	    at the Gaviota siding, this section stood as a classic
	    searchlight-signaled APB gap until UP completely converted
	    it to bi-directional CTC and upgraded the lineside
	    infrastructure in the mid-to-late 2000s.

	2. Oxnard to Montalvo

	 -  The Segment: A short but historically unique 5-mile APB
	    application existed on the Coast Line between Oxnard and
	    Montalvo.
	 -  SP's Special Rules: On this piece, SP specifically
	    implemented APB rules that allowed the wayside signals to
	    directly authorize train movements (superseding timetable
	    and train order rights) to handle traffic transitioning
	    from the yard at Oxnard and the junction with the Santa
	    Paula Branch at Montalvo.
	 -  Conversion: This bottleneck area was wrapped into wider
	    regional Southern California signaling projects and fully
	    modernized into standard CTC by the late 20th century under
	    SP and Metrolink/UP coordination.
